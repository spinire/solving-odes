What an ODE solver does

Often what you know about a quantity is how fast it changes, not the quantity itself. A differential equation records that rule, and a solver turns it back into numbers.

Why you'd care 2 min

A patient is given 400 mg of a drug. How much is left in the blood 8 hours later? Nobody measures that directly. What pharmacologists know is a rule about how fast the drug leaves: the more there is, the faster the body clears it. For this drug, at every moment the amount falls at a rate of $0.13$ times the amount present, per hour.

Rules like this are everywhere: populations, falling objects, outbreaks. A rule about rates, written as an equation, is a differential equation. This module is about what such an equation tells you, how to picture it, and what job a computer does when it “solves” one.

Lesson 1: Rules about rates 5 min

Write $y(t)$ for the amount of drug, in mg, $t$ hours after the dose. Its derivative $y'(t)$ is the rate at which the amount changes, in mg per hour. The rule says this rate is proportional to the amount, that is, a fixed number ($0.13$) times the amount, and negative, because the amount is going down:

$$y' = -0.13\,y .$$

Right after the dose, $y = 400$, so the amount is falling at $0.13 \times 400 = 52$ mg per hour. Later, with less drug in the blood, it falls more slowly. The same equation, $y' = -r\,y$ with a positive constant $r$, describes anything that decays at a rate proportional to the amount present, such as a radioactive substance; here $r = 0.13$.

Every equation in these modules has the same shape:

$$y' = f(t, y).$$

The function $f$ is a slope machine: give it a time $t$ and a value $y$, and it returns the rate of change at that moment. For the drug, $f(t, y) = -0.13\,y$; it happens not to use $t$. Such an equation is called an ordinary differential equation, or ODE: “ordinary” because the unknown $y$ depends on one variable, time.

Four rules about rates
SituationThe rule in wordsThe equation
Drug in the blood, $y$ in mgFalls at a rate proportional to the amount$y' = -0.13\,y$
Fish in a pond, $y$ = number of fishGrows in proportion to its size, but growth slows to zero as $y$ nears 100$y' = 0.5\,y\,(1 - y/100)$
Skydiver, speed $v$ in m/sGravity adds 9.8 m/s every second; air resistance takes away a part proportional to the speed$v' = 9.8 - 0.2\,v$
Coffee, temperature $y$ in °CCools at a rate proportional to how much hotter it is than the 20° room$y' = -0.1\,(y - 20)$

Look at the fish. When $y$ is small, $1 - y/100$ is close to 1, so $y' \approx 0.5\,y$: plain growth. When $y$ is close to 100, the factor $1 - y/100$ is close to 0, so growth almost stops. The equation builds in the food shortage. It is a version of the logistic equation, the standard model of a population limited by its food supply.

What a solution is

A solution is a function whose derivative obeys the rule at every time. For the drug, try $y(t) = 400\,e^{-0.13t}$. Its derivative is

$$\begin{aligned} y'(t) &= 400 \times (-0.13)\, e^{-0.13t} \\ &= -0.13 \times \big(400\,e^{-0.13t}\big) = -0.13\, y(t), \end{aligned}$$

so it obeys the rule. But $y(t) = 200\,e^{-0.13t}$ does too, and so does any number times $e^{-0.13t}$. An equation about rates has many solutions. Lesson 3 explains how to pick the one you want.

Lesson 2: Pictures of slopes 7 min

The slope machine gives a slope at every point $(t, y)$. That slope is how steeply a solution must be climbing or falling if it passes through that point. Draw a short line segment with that slope at many points and you get a slope field (also called a direction field). The equation is now a picture.

A solution curve has to match the segments everywhere it goes, so it flows through the field like a leaf carried by a stream. Pick a starting point and the field tells the curve where to go next, and next, and next.

Explore

Try this

  1. Choose the fish. Draw the solution that starts at $y(0) = 10$. Describe its shape.

    Answer

    An S-shape. It grows slowly at first (few fish), fastest around $y = 50$, then levels off just under 100 as food runs short.

  2. Still with the fish, start at $y(0) = 100$, and then at $y(0) = 140$. What happens?

    What happened?

    Starting at 100, the curve is flat: $f(t, 100) = 0.5 \times 100 \times (1 - 1) = 0$, so the population never changes; the next section looks at such constant solutions. Starting at 140, there are more fish than the pond can feed, $1 - y/100$ is negative, and the population falls back toward 100.

  3. For all three equations, the segments along any horizontal line are parallel. Why?

    Answer

    None of the three slope machines uses $t$: the slope depends only on the height $y$. When $f$ does use $t$, the segments change as you move left or right too.

Flat lines: steady states

Sometimes the slope is zero all along a horizontal line. For the fish, $f(t, 100) = 0.5 \times 100 \times (1 - 100/100) = 0$ at every time $t$, so every segment on the line $y = 100$ is flat. A solution that starts there has nothing pushing it up or down, and stays at 100 forever. A constant solution like this is called a steady-state solution, or steady state for short.

To find the steady states, set the right-hand side equal to zero and solve for $y$. For the fish, $0.5\,y\,(1 - y/100) = 0$ gives $y = 0$ (no fish, so none are born) or $y = 100$. For the skydiver, $9.8 - 0.2\,v = 0$ gives $v = 49$ m/s, the speed at which air resistance exactly cancels gravity. In the explorer, solutions that start near $y = 100$ or $v = 49$ settle onto them: these steady states are asymptotically stable. Solutions that start near $y = 0$ for the fish move away from it: that steady state is unstable.

Quick check

Lesson 3: One curve, and the solver's job 3 min

A slope field has a solution curve through every point, so the equation alone doesn't say which curve you mean. A starting value does. An initial value problem is a differential equation together with the value at the start:

$$y' = f(t, y), \qquad y(0) = \alpha .$$

The Greek letter $\alpha$ (“alpha”) stands for the starting value.

For the drug, $\alpha = 400$: the condition $y(0) = 400$ picks out $y(t) = 400\,e^{-0.13t}$, and the answer to the opening question is $y(8) = 400\,e^{-1.04} \approx 141$ mg.

Here calculus produced a formula. For most equations that come from real problems, such as the outbreak model in Module 5 or a swinging pendulum, there is no formula for the solution in terms of familiar functions. That is where a numerical solver comes in. Its job:

  • You give it the slope machine $f$, the starting value $\alpha$, and how far to go, say up to time $T$.
  • It gives back a table of times $t_0 = 0, t_1, t_2, \dots, t_M = T$, with an approximate value $y_0 = \alpha, y_1, y_2, \dots$ at each one.

The solver never sees the solution curve. All it can do is ask $f$ for the slope at points it chooses, and build the table from the answers, much like finding your way in fog with only a compass. Module 2 shows the simplest way to do it. The questions that matter afterwards are how accurate the table is, and how much work it takes.

Takeaways

  • A differential equation $y' = f(t, y)$ is a rule about rates: $f$ gives the slope at every time $t$ and value $y$.
  • A solution is a function whose slope obeys the rule at every time. There are many; a starting value $y(0) = \alpha$ picks one. Together they make an initial value problem.
  • A slope field draws the rule as short segments; solution curves follow them. Where the slope is zero for all $t$, there is a constant solution, a steady state.
  • A numerical solver takes $f$, $\alpha$ and an end time, and returns a table of approximate values. It only ever asks $f$ for slopes.
