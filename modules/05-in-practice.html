ODE solvers in practice

Real models have several quantities changing together. The methods you know already handle them; this module shows how, and what the ODE solvers in scientific software add on top.

Why you'd care 2 min

A new infection appears in a city. Public-health officials want to know: how many people will be ill at the peak, and when? How much would it help if people met less often, or if illness were shorter?

No single equation answers this, because three quantities change together. The number of people who can still catch the disease shrinks as people fall ill. The number of ill people grows when the ill meet the susceptible, and shrinks as people recover. The number recovered grows. Each rate depends on the others. A model like this is a system of differential equations, and solving systems is what ODE solvers spend most of their time doing.

Lesson 1: Systems: an outbreak 9 min

The SIR model

Split the population into three groups, measured as fractions of the whole population: $S(t)$, those susceptible to the disease; $I(t)$, those ill (and infectious); and $R(t)$, those recovered. With time $t$ in days, a standard model is

$$\begin{aligned} S' &= -aSI, \\ I' &= -bI + aSI, \\ R' &= bI. \end{aligned}$$

Read it term by term:

  • $aSI$: new infections. They need a susceptible person to meet an ill one, and the number of such meetings is proportional to both $S$ and $I$, so to their product. The constant $a$ is the infection rate. These people leave $S$ and join $I$.
  • $bI$: recoveries. Each day a fixed fraction $b$ of the ill recover, so the illness lasts about $1/b$ days on average. The constant $b$ is the recovery rate. These people leave $I$ and join $R$.

Add the three equations: $S' + I' + R' = 0$. Nobody is created or lost, so $S + I + R$ stays equal to 1, the whole population. That makes a handy check on any computed solution. This is an example of an SIR model.

Vectors make it one equation

Collect the unknowns into a vector, written in bold, and do the same for the right-hand sides:

$$\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} S \\ I \\ R \end{pmatrix},$$
$$\mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{y}) = \begin{pmatrix} -aSI \\ -bI + aSI \\ bI \end{pmatrix}.$$

Then the whole system is $\mathbf{y}' = \mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{y})$ with $\mathbf{y}(0) = \boldsymbol{\alpha}$, the vector of starting values. That is exactly the shape of every problem in Modules 1–4, and every method carries over unchanged, with vectors in place of numbers. Euler's method becomes

$$\mathbf{y}_{j+1} = \mathbf{y}_j + k\, \mathbf{f}(t_j, \mathbf{y}_j),$$

and RK4 is the same as before with $\mathbf{k}_1, \dots, \mathbf{k}_4$ now vectors. Orders and stability properties carry over too. The one thing to be careful about: compute every component of $\mathbf{f}$ from the old values $\mathbf{y}_j$, and only then update all the components together.

A worked Euler step. Take $a = 0.3$ and $b = 0.1$ per day (an illness lasting about 10 days), and start with 1% of the population ill: $S = 0.99$, $I = 0.01$, $R = 0$. With a step of $k = 1$ day:

One Euler step for the SIR model with k = 1 day
componentslope from the old valuesnew value
$S$$-0.3 \times 0.99 \times 0.01 = -0.00297$$0.99 - 0.00297 = 0.98703$
$I$$-0.1 \times 0.01 + 0.00297 = 0.00197$$0.01 + 0.00197 = 0.01197$
$R$$0.1 \times 0.01 = 0.001$$0 + 0.001 = 0.001$

Check: $0.98703 + 0.01197 + 0.001 = 1$. The ill fraction grew by about 20% in one day.

Explore

Try this

  1. With $a = 0.3$ and $b = 0.1$, when is the peak, and what fraction of the population never falls ill?

    Answer

    About 30% are ill at the peak, around day 27. By the end, only about 6% have never been infected: almost everyone catches it.

  2. Lower the infection rate $a$ (people meet less often). What happens to the size and timing of the peak?

    Answer

    The peak gets lower and later, and more people never fall ill. With $a = 0.2$, the peak is about 16% around day 44, and 20% never fall ill. This is what “flattening the curve” means.

  3. Set $a = 0.1$ and $b = 0.1$. Why does the outbreak never take off?

    Answer

    $I' = I\,(aS - b)$, so the ill fraction grows only while $aS > b$, that is, while $S > b/a$. Here $b/a = 1$ and $S$ starts below 1, so $I$ shrinks from the start. The ratio $a/b$ is the average number of people one ill person infects in a fully susceptible population; an outbreak needs it to be more than 1.

Lesson 2: Second-order equations 4 min

Newton's second law, force = mass × acceleration, gives equations with a second derivative: if $y(t)$ is a position, then

$$m\,y'' = F(t, y, y'),$$
$$y(0) = \alpha, \quad y'(0) = \beta .$$

The force $F$ may depend on time, position and velocity. To start, you need both the position $\alpha$ and the velocity $\beta$. The methods you know only handle first derivatives, but there is a standard trick: give the velocity its own name, $v = y'$. Then $v' = y''$, and the one second-order equation becomes two first-order ones:

$$\begin{aligned} y' &= v, \\ v' &= \tfrac{1}{m} F(t, y, v), \end{aligned}$$
$$\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} y \\ v \end{pmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol{\alpha} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix}.$$

Now it is a system $\mathbf{y}' = \mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{y})$, and Euler, Heun and RK4 all apply.

Example: a pendulum. The angle $\theta(t)$ of a swinging pendulum obeys $\theta'' = -\sin\theta$ (in suitable units). With $\omega = \theta'$, the angular velocity, this becomes

$$\theta' = \omega, \qquad \omega' = -\sin\theta .$$

There is no formula for the solution in terms of familiar functions, but a solver handles it easily.

Go deeper: very long simulations

A pendulum without friction should swing with the same amplitude forever, but RK4's small errors make the amplitude drift slowly over thousands of swings. For problems where energy must be conserved over very long times (pendulums, planetary orbits, molecules), special symplectic methods are used instead.

Lesson 3: What library solvers do 7 min

In practice you rarely write your own RK4 loop. Scientific software (Python's SciPy, MATLAB, Julia, R) has ODE solvers that you call with the slope function $\mathbf{f}$, the starting values $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ and the time interval. Inside, they use the ideas from Modules 2–4, plus two more.

1. They choose the step for you

A fixed step wastes work: it has to be small enough for the hardest part of the solution, even where the solution is boring. An adaptive solver changes the step as it goes. At each step it estimates its own error, for example by comparing two methods of different order on the same step, an idea like the $k$ versus $k/2$ check in Module 2. If the error estimate is too large, it rejects the step and tries again with a smaller one; if it is comfortably small, it accepts the step and tries a larger one next time.

The upshot is that you choose a tolerance, not a step: you say how accurate you want the answer, and the solver works out the steps. The tolerance controls the error made in each step; the final error is usually similar in size but not guaranteed.

Go deeper: what is inside a library solver

The usual first choice, and the default in SciPy and MATLAB (called RK45 and ode45), is a pair of explicit Runge–Kutta methods of orders 4 and 5 that share their slope evaluations: the difference between their two answers is the error estimate. Solvers also give values at whatever times you ask for, filling in between their own steps by interpolation.

2. They offer stiff methods

Explicit methods are only conditionally A-stable (Module 3), so on a stiff problem an explicit solver crawls along with a huge number of tiny steps. Every library also offers implicit methods for stiff problems (BDF and Radau methods; in MATLAB, ode15s). If a solver crawls on a problem whose solution looks smooth, try one.

Before you trust the answer

  • Tighten the tolerance (or halve the step) and solve again. If the answer changes in digits you care about, it was not accurate enough.
  • Check what must be true: $S + I + R = 1$ in the SIR model, energy for a pendulum without friction, populations that cannot go negative, the steady states you expect.

Takeaways

  • A system $\mathbf{y}' = \mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{y})$, $\mathbf{y}(0) = \boldsymbol{\alpha}$, has the same shape as a single equation, and every method carries over with vectors. Compute all of $\mathbf{f}$ from the old values, then update.
  • The SIR model: $S' = -aSI$, $I' = -bI + aSI$, $R' = bI$. The ill fraction grows only while $S > b/a$, and $S + I + R$ stays constant.
  • A second-order equation becomes a first-order system by naming the velocity, $v = y'$.
  • Library solvers adapt the step to meet a tolerance you choose, and offer implicit methods for stiff problems.
  • Check an answer by tightening the tolerance and by testing what must be true.
