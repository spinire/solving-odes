Euler's method

A differential equation tells you how fast something is changing. Euler's method turns that into the thing itself: follow the slope for a short step, then look again.

Why you'd care 2 min

A cup of coffee at $90^\circ$C is left in a $20^\circ$C room. It cools faster when it is much hotter than the room, and more slowly as it gets close. Newton's law of cooling makes this precise: if $y(t)$ is the temperature after $t$ minutes, then

$$y'(t) = -0.1\,\big(y(t) - 20\big),$$
$$y(0) = 90.$$

At the start the coffee is $70^\circ$ above the room, so it cools at $0.1 \times 70 = 7$ degrees per minute. The equation tells you how fast the temperature is changing at every moment. It does not tell you the temperature at, say, $t = 10$.

This equation happens to have a formula for its solution, which we will use to check our answers. Most equations that matter do not: an outbreak model, a pendulum with friction, a drug the body clears at a rate that depends on the dose. For those, a computer builds the solution one small step at a time. Euler's method is the simplest way to do it, and every better method starts from the same idea.

Lesson 1: Follow the slope 6 min

As in Module 1, the problem is $y' = f(t, y)$, $y(0) = \alpha$, where the slope machine $f$ gives the rate of change at any time and value. For the coffee, $f(t, y) = -0.1\,(y - 20)$, $\alpha = 90$, and $f(0, 90) = -7$.

Now suppose the coffee kept cooling at 7 degrees per minute for one whole minute. Its temperature would be $90 - 7 = 83$. That is not exactly right, because the cooling slows down during that minute. But over a short time the rate barely changes, so 83 is close. Then do the same again from the new point: work out the slope at $83^\circ$, and follow it for another minute.

That is all of Euler's method. Pick a step $k$ and the times $t_j = jk$, that is $t_0 = 0,\ t_1 = k,\ t_2 = 2k$, and so on. Write $y_j$ for the computed value at $t_j$; it approximates the true value $y(t_j)$. To go up to a time $T$ takes $M = T/k$ steps, so the last time is $t_M = T$. Starting from $y_0 = \alpha$, repeat

$$y_{j+1} = y_j + k\, f(t_j, y_j).$$

In words: new value = old value + step × slope at the old point. In a picture, each step walks along the tangent line for a time $k$. Because the formula gives $y_{j+1}$ directly from values already known, Euler's method is called an explicit method.

Where the formula comes from. The derivative $y'(t)$ is the limit of the slope $\big(y(t+k) - y(t)\big)/k$ as $k \to 0$. A computer cannot take the limit, so it uses a small $k$ instead. This is the forward difference:

$$y'(t_j) \approx \frac{y(t_{j+1}) - y(t_j)}{k}.$$

The differential equation says $y'(t_j) = f\big(t_j, y(t_j)\big)$. Put the two together and solve for the value at the next time:

$$y(t_{j+1}) \approx y(t_j) + k\, f\big(t_j, y(t_j)\big).$$

Replace the true values by the computed ones and you have Euler's method. So everything you know about the forward difference, including how large its error is, carries over. Lesson 3 uses that.

Lesson 3: How big is the error? 7 min

Each step makes a small error, and the errors add up over the steps. To see how the total depends on $k$, count both.

The error in one step

Taylor's theorem gives the error of the forward difference:

$$y'(t_j) = \frac{y(t_{j+1}) - y(t_j)}{k} + \tau_j,$$
$$\tau_j = -\tfrac12 k\, y''(t_j) + \cdots .$$

The number $\tau_j$ is the truncation error: what Euler's method throws away at step $j$. It comes from $y''$, the bending of the true curve, which is what the picture in Lesson 1 showed: a straight step cannot follow a curve. For small $k$, $\tau_j$ is roughly proportional to $k$. We write $\tau_j = O(k)$ and say Euler's method is first order.

Adding up the steps

Since $y'(t_j) = f\big(t_j, y(t_j)\big)$, multiplying the equation above by $k$ and solving for $y(t_{j+1})$ gives

$$y(t_{j+1}) = y(t_j) + k\, f\big(t_j, y(t_j)\big) - k\,\tau_j .$$

The first two terms are exactly Euler's step; the last term, $k\,\tau_j$, is what the step leaves out. It is $k$ times something proportional to $k$, so each step adds an error of about $C k^2$, where the number $C$ depends on how much the solution bends. To reach $t = 10$ takes $M = 10/k$ steps. Altogether, the error at $t = 10$ is about

$$\underbrace{\frac{10}{k}}_{M \text{ steps}} \times \underbrace{C k^2}_{\text{per step}} \;=\; 10\,C\,k .$$

This count is rough: an error made early can grow or shrink in later steps. But for well-behaved problems it gives the right answer. The error at a fixed time is roughly proportional to $k$. Halve the step and you about halve the error. Here are the coffee's errors at $t = 10$:

Every halving of $k$ multiplies the error by about $\tfrac12$. On a log-log plot, error against step, the points lie on a line of slope 1. A method with error proportional to $k^2$ would give slope 2.

What first order costs

To make the error 10 times smaller you need a step 10 times smaller, which means 10 times as many steps. Each extra correct digit costs a factor of 10 in work. With $k = 0.125$ the coffee is right to about $0.16$ degrees after 80 steps; getting it right to $0.0016$ would take about 8,000. That is why Module 4 looks for methods whose error shrinks much faster as $k$ gets smaller.

Go deeper: checking accuracy when there is no formula

For most real problems there is no exact solution to compare with. Run Euler twice, with step $k$ and with step $k/2$. Since the error is proportional to the step, the run with $k/2$ is off by about half as much as the run with $k$, so the difference between the two runs is about the error of the $k/2$ run.

For the coffee: $k = 1$ gives $44.407$ and $k = 0.5$ gives $45.094$. They differ by $0.69$, and the true error of the $k = 0.5$ run is $0.66$. Not exact, but a good guide to how many digits you can trust.

Takeaways

  • An initial value problem $y' = f(t, y)$, $y(0) = \alpha$ gives the slope at every point and the starting value.
  • Euler's method: $y_{j+1} = y_j + k\, f(t_j, y_j)$. Follow the slope at the start of the step for a time $k$, then repeat.
  • It is the forward difference, rearranged. Its truncation error $\tau_j$ is $O(k)$: Euler's method is first order.
  • The error at a fixed time is roughly proportional to $k$. Halving the step about halves the error and doubles the work.
