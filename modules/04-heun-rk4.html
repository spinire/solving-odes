Better slopes: Heun and RK4

Euler's method uses one slope per step, taken at the start. Using a few well-chosen slopes per step instead makes the error shrink dramatically faster as the step gets smaller.

Why you'd care 2 min

Module 2 showed that Euler's method is first order: to get one more correct digit you need ten times as many steps. Here is the coffee, $y' = -0.1\,(y - 20)$, $y(0) = 90$, solved up to $t = 10$ minutes with 10 steps of $k = 1$ minute, by Euler's method and by the two methods of this module:

Same problem, same 10 steps. RK4 is about 60,000 times more accurate than Euler. To match it, Euler's method would need more than half a million steps. Each RK4 step does four times the work of an Euler step, not 50,000 times. This module shows where these methods come from and why they win.

Lesson 1: Two slopes: Heun's method 8 min

A step is an integral

Integrate the differential equation $y' = f(t, y)$ from $t_j$ to $t_{j+1}$. By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus,

$$y(t_{j+1}) - y(t_j) = \int_{t_j}^{t_{j+1}} f\big(t, y(t)\big)\, dt .$$

So the change in $y$ over one step is the area under the graph of the slope, $f\big(t, y(t)\big)$, between $t_j$ and $t_{j+1}$. Every method in this module comes from approximating that area with an integration rule.

Euler's method uses the simplest rule: a rectangle whose height is the slope at the left end, $t_j$. That gives $y_{j+1} = y_j + k\,f(t_j, y_j)$. A rectangle ignores how the slope changes during the step, which is why Euler is only first order.

The trapezoid rule, and its catch

The trapezoid rule averages the heights at both ends, and is much more accurate. With $f_j = f(t_j, y_j)$ for short, it gives the trapezoidal method

$$y_{j+1} = y_j + \frac{k}{2}\big(f_j + f_{j+1}\big),$$
$$f_{j+1} = f(t_{j+1}, y_{j+1}).$$

The catch is the slope at the end, $f_{j+1}$: it needs $y_{j+1}$, the very value being computed. Like backward Euler in Module 3, the trapezoidal method is implicit, so each step means solving an equation.

Heun's method: predict, then correct

A simple way around the catch: first predict the end value with an ordinary Euler step. Call the prediction $\hat y_{j+1}$ (“y-hat”):

$$\hat y_{j+1} = y_j + k f_j .$$

Then use the prediction to evaluate the slope at the end, and step with the average of the two slopes. This is Heun's method:

$$y_{j+1} = y_j + \frac{k}{2}\Big(f(t_j, y_j) + f\big(t_{j+1},\, \hat y_{j+1}\big)\Big).$$

It is explicit again, because everything on the right is known. Each step costs two evaluations of $f$ instead of one.

A worked step. For the coffee with $k = 1$:

One Heun step for the coffee with k = 1

The true value is $y(1) = 83.3386$. Euler's $83$ was off by $0.34$; Heun's $83.35$ is off by $0.011$.

How accurate? Heun's truncation error is $\tau_j = O(k^2)$: it is second order. Halving the step divides the error by about $2^2 = 4$. Heun's method is one of a family of second-order methods called RK2 methods (Runge–Kutta methods of order 2).

Lesson 2: Four slopes: RK4 8 min

A better integration rule gives a better method. Simpson's rule uses the heights at the start, the middle and the end of the interval, with weights $1, 4, 1$. The method built this way is the classic RK4 method, the workhorse of ODE solvers. It takes four slope samples per step and combines them:

$$y_{j+1} = y_j + \frac16\big(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4\big),$$

where

$$\begin{aligned} k_1 &= k\, f(t_j,\; y_j), \\ k_2 &= k\, f\big(t_j + \tfrac{k}{2},\; y_j + \tfrac12 k_1\big), \\ k_3 &= k\, f\big(t_j + \tfrac{k}{2},\; y_j + \tfrac12 k_2\big), \\ k_4 &= k\, f(t_{j+1},\; y_j + k_3). \end{aligned}$$

Careful with the notation. $k$ on its own is the step size. The numbers $k_1, k_2, k_3, k_4$ are not step sizes: each is the step times a slope, so each is a change in $y$, the change that a whole step at that slope would make. Read them like this:

  1. $k_1$: the change using the slope at the start (this is exactly Euler's change).
  2. $k_2$: go half a step using $k_1$, measure the slope there at the midpoint, and turn it into a change.
  3. $k_3$: go half a step again, this time using the better estimate $k_2$, and measure the midpoint slope again.
  4. $k_4$: go a whole step using $k_3$ and measure the slope at the end.

The final step is a weighted average of the four changes. The two midpoint samples share Simpson's weight of 4, which is why each gets a 2.

A worked step. For the coffee with $k = 1$, where $f(t, y) = -0.1\,(y - 20)$. With $k = 1$, each change is just the slope, so the multiplication by $k$ is left out:

How accurate? The classic RK4 method has truncation error $\tau_j = O(k^4)$: it is fourth order. Halving the step divides the error by about $2^4 = 16$, and dividing the step by 10 divides it by $10^4 = 10{,}000$.

What RK4 does not fix

Like Euler, these explicit methods are only conditionally A-stable: Heun needs $k < 2/r$, RK4 needs $k < 2.785/r$. So stiff problems still call for the implicit methods of Module 3; for everything else, RK4 is usually the first choice.

Takeaways

  • A step is an integral: $y(t_{j+1}) - y(t_j) = \int_{t_j}^{t_{j+1}} f\,dt$. Euler uses a rectangle; better integration rules give better methods.
  • The trapezoidal method is second order but implicit. Heun's method predicts the end value with an Euler step and averages the two slopes: explicit, second order, two evaluations of $f$ per step.
  • Classic RK4 combines four slope samples with weights $1, 2, 2, 1$ (Simpson's rule): $y_{j+1} = y_j + \tfrac16(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4)$. It is fourth order and costs four evaluations of $f$ per step. Each $k_i$ is a change in $y$, not a step size.
  • Compare methods at equal work. Higher order wins by more and more as you ask for more accuracy.
  • Explicit Runge–Kutta methods are conditionally A-stable (RK4: $k < 2.785/r$), so stiff problems still need implicit methods.
